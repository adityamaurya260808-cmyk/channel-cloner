Only one person knows. Grandpa. He doesn’t laugh. He just nods. A whole year alone? Then don’t go in empty-handed. Take a song. His song. The same old disco record he dances to every Sunday, tapping the counter. He even taught you the steps. Boom. Boom. Boom. About a hundred beats a minute. You hum it inside every frozen year, so the silence isn’t silent. And whenever you worry, he says the same thing. A second is plenty, Leo... if you use it. Sunday. The kitchen. The song. Grandpa falls. And you hold your breath. Now you’re in the year. A body takes about half a second to reach the floor. You watch every centimeter. What can a frozen boy do? Nothing. Not yet. You remember a first-aid class. When a heart suddenly stops, the brain can start to be damaged after just a few minutes without oxygen. Every real second counts. And you have a whole year... to think about that. Can you even remember the steps? You go over that class again. And again. A thousand times. But in here, nothing gets worse. Grandpa’s still standing, sort of. Nothing ends. You could stay. A year. Ten years. Forever. In here, he’s still okay. Out there... the clock starts. Stay in the frozen second... or step out and fight for him. Then you hear it. One long note. His song. A second is plenty... if you use it. You’ve had your year. Now use the second. You breathe out. The spoon hits the floor. Mom! Call an ambulance! You kneel. Hands in the center of his chest. Push hard. About five centimeters deep. About a hundred to a hundred and twenty times a minute. How fast is that? You already know. Boom. Boom. Boom. His song is still playing. You push on every beat. Your arms burn. You don’t stop. Nine minutes later, sirens. The paramedics take over with a defibrillator. Then... a beep. A heartbeat. Who kept that rhythm going? Me. And his song. The next day, Grandpa smiles from a hospital bed. So. How long did that second feel? A year. And the nine minutes after? You squeeze his hand. Like nine minutes. Grandpa winks. Told you. A second is plenty. And for the first time in forty years... you’re not alone. That night, you hold your breath, just to rest. And in the frozen picture, something... moves. Someone else is in the year with you. Is any of this real? People often say time slowed down during a scare. In 2007, neuroscientist David Eagleman tested it. Volunteers fell about thirty meters into a net while watching a display flash numbers too fast to read. If time really slowed, they could read them. They couldn’t. Their brains just recorded more. Frightening moments get stored in richer detail, so they feel longer afterward. And the CPR part? That’s real.
