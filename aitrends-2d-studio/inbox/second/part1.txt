Sunday afternoon. Music on. Your grandpa is dancing in the kitchen, the way he always does. Then his hand slips off the counter. His knees fold. And the whole world... stops. His glasses hang in the air. A spoon floats above the floor. The music holds one long note. Nothing moves. Not even you. You’re standing inside a single second. And you’re about to live a whole year in it. Three weeks earlier, it happened for the very first time. Your name is Leo. You’re fifteen. A year holds about thirty-one and a half million seconds. Most people feel each one as... just a second. You? Hold your breath, and one second stretches into a full year. A year to think. To remember. To plan. But there’s a rule. Your mind runs free. Your body doesn’t move. Not a finger. Not even your eyes. You can think anything... but you can’t do anything. Not until you breathe out. So what if one second felt like a year? The first time is a maths test. Question seven. You hold your breath... and you get a whole year with it. You solve it. Then you solve it three more ways. Then you get bored and invent a board game in your head. You breathe out. The clock hasn’t moved. Leo, you finished in four minutes? I had... plenty of time. Plus: chess club. Every move gets a year of thought. You beat the school champion in eleven moves. Your best friend Jake stares at you. How long were you thinking? About a second. Mate. That was a very scary second. Then football. A hard penalty reaches the goal in about half a second. You hold your breath as the ball leaves the boot. You see the spin. The angle. The exact corner. But the rule still holds. You can’t dive any faster than any other keeper. You just dive the right way... first time. Fingertips. Saved. Minus: your eyes can’t move. So for a whole year, you stare at one frozen picture. Sometimes it’s a sunset. Sometimes it’s Jake... mid-sneeze. For three hundred and sixty-five days. Your heart beats roughly once a second. So inside the year, you hear one heartbeat. Just one. Like a door closing very, very slowly. But where it gets really crazy... is a storm. A lightning flash is over in less than a second. You get to watch it crawl across the sky... for months. And there’s a cost. Every time you use it, you live another year. Alone. No voices. No touch. Just your thoughts and one frozen picture. You’re fifteen. But you’ve used it forty times. Which means... you’ve lived forty years nobody saw. The price? Scientists know long isolation is hard on the human brain. People kept alone for long stretches often report anxiety, confusion, even seeing things that aren’t there. You start to feel it. Jake notices. Dude. You sigh like my dad. And one question keeps growing. What if one day... you can’t make yourself breathe out?
