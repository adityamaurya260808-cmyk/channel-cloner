You open your eyes. The ceiling has glow-in-the-dark stars on it. You haven’t seen those stars... in twenty years. Your feet don’t reach the end of the bed. Your hands are small. Your voice cracks when you say hello. Then a voice shouts from the kitchen. Eli! Pancakes! You’re going to be late for school! School? Yesterday you were thirty-two. You had a mortgage, a bad back, and a job you didn’t love. Today... you’re twelve. And you remember everything. Every exam answer. Every mistake. Every person you lost... along the way. Most people remember almost nothing from before age three or four. And by thirty, the memories of being twelve are blurry, rebuilt every time you recall them. You? You have twenty extra years of memories... in a twelve-year-old head. But there’s a rule. You find it on the first morning. You warn Mom that the bathroom pipe will burst on Saturday. She checks it. It’s cracked. She fixes it. And that night... you can’t remember the color of your first car. Every time you change something, the memory it replaced disappears. Change something small, lose something small. Change something big... and your whole future could vanish. So what if you woke up at twelve with all your memories? Plus: school becomes very easy. You’ve done algebra. You’ve done taxes. Your teacher, Ms. Reyes, hands back your test. One hundred percent? Eli... did you study? For about twenty years, yes. Minus: you forgot long division. Completely. Turns out adults just use a calculator. Money? Everyone thinks you’d be rich. But you never memorized lottery numbers. Who does that? What you do remember is maths. Save money that grows seven percent a year, and it roughly doubles every ten years. Start at twelve... and your coins have decades to grow. So you open a savings jar. Mom is very confused. Languages? Kids pick up accents far more easily than adults do. Your adult self spent three years failing Spanish. Your twelve-year-old mouth gets it in three months. Plus: you finally understand your parents. You see Mom checking the bills at midnight. So you quietly do the dishes. Every night. Mom stares at you. Who are you, and what have you done with my son? Then the hard parts start. Bedtime is nine o’clock. You argue like a lawyer. But a twelve-year-old body needs about nine to twelve hours of sleep. You fall asleep... in the middle of your own argument. And your feelings? Out of control. The part of the brain that handles self-control keeps developing until your mid-twenties. Yours is twelve. So you know better... and you still cry when you lose a video game. Your best friend Omar squints at you. Dude. You’ve been weird all week. But where it gets really strange... is knowing what’s coming. You know which teacher will leave. You know which song will be huge next summer. You know Mom will change jobs in two years... and be happier. You stay quiet about that one. It’s better as a surprise. Each time you fix something, another memory slips. So you stop fixing the small stuff. And there’s a cost. The price? You’re lonely in a way nobody can see. You can’t tell anyone. Who would believe you? Your friends talk about cartoons. You want to talk about rent. Your mind is thirty-two. Your life is twelve. And every small change you make steals another memory. Your first apartment. Your college roommate’s name. The taste of your favorite food from the future. You start to wonder... how much of your old self will be left? And there’s one thing you came back to fix. One day you’ve never forgiven yourself for. You whisper it to yourself every night. You didn’t come back to win. You came back to fix it. Omar has been your best friend since you were six. He laughs too loud. He shares his lunch whether you want it or not. And you two have a secret signal. Two taps on the desk... then a thumbs-up. It means: I’ve got your back. Omar notices everything. You talk like my uncle now. Is that a bad thing? Kind of. He sells insurance. In your old life, you stopped using the signal at twelve. Because last week, someone pulled the school fire alarm. It was you. A dare. Omar was standing next to it. The teachers caught him. And you said... nothing. On Friday, there’s a hearing. In your old life, Omar was expelled. His family moved away. You never spoke again. And there’s Dad. Dad works nights at the hospital laundry. Every Sunday he calls from his break. Hey, kiddo. Just calling. No reason. In your old life, you stopped picking up. Your phone filled with those messages. No reason. No reason. No reason. You never once called back. Friday comes. The principal’s office smells like old coffee and floor polish. Omar sits in the chair by the window. His mom holds his hand. His eyes are red. Principal Hart folds her hands. Someone pulled that alarm. Three hundred students went outside in the rain. If nobody speaks... Omar is expelled. And then she smiles at you. Eli, on a happier note... you’ve been picked for the city scholarship. Your stomach drops. You remember this moment. In your old life, that scholarship opened every door. The top school. The top university. The good job. The quiet apartment. The phone you never answered. If you confess, the scholarship is gone. Rules are rules. And a change this big... could wipe out every memory you brought back. All twenty years. Every answer. Every advantage. You’d just be... twelve. The clock on the wall ticks. Every second, the old future pulls you back. Omar looks at you. He taps the desk twice. Then he stops. He doesn’t finish the signal. He doesn’t think you’ll help. Why would he? Last time... you didn’t. Your perfect future... or your best friend. You think about the quiet apartment. The messages. No reason. No reason. You didn’t come back to win. You came back to fix it. You stand up. It was me. Silence. I pulled the alarm. On a dare. Omar was just standing there. Omar’s mouth falls open. Principal Hart takes a long breath. Eli... you understand what this means for the scholarship? I do. Two weeks of detention. One letter home. No scholarship. Omar stays. In the hallway, he grabs your shoulder. Why? You could’ve said nothing. I already did that once, you say. He doesn’t understand. He doesn’t need to. He taps the wall twice... and gives you a thumbs-up. The next morning, the whole class has heard. Some kids think you’re crazy. Most think you’re brave. That night, it starts. Your memories begin to fade. Your old office. Your old street. The faces of people you haven’t met yet. You grab a notebook and write as fast as you can. ‘Keep Omar. Answer Dad. Learn Spanish. Save the coins.’ By morning... you can’t remember why you wrote it. You’re just twelve. Normal. Kind of behind in maths. Then on Sunday, the phone rings. Hey, kiddo. Just calling. No reason. You don’t know why your eyes sting. You just hold the phone a little tighter. Hey, Dad. Tell me everything. And somewhere deep down... something feels fixed. Is any of this real? Nobody can wake up in their younger body. But some parts are real. Your memories aren’t stored like videos. Every time you remember something, your brain rebuilds it, and it can change a little. The part of the brain behind self-control really does keep developing into your mid-twenties. And in 1995, psychologists Thomas Gilovich and Victoria Medvec published research on regret. Over the long run, people regret what they didn’t do more than what they did. Like staying quiet. Like not calling back. So if you could go back... what’s the one thing you’d fix? What do you think?
