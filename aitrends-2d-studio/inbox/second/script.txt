Sunday afternoon. Music on. Your grandpa is dancing in the kitchen, the way he always does. Then his hand slips off the counter. His knees fold. And the whole world... stops. His glasses hang in the air. A spoon floats above the floor. The music holds one long note. Nothing moves. Not even you. You’re standing inside a single second. And you’re about to live a whole year in it. Three weeks earlier, it happened for the very first time. Your name is Leo. You’re fifteen. A year holds about thirty-one and a half million seconds. Most people feel each one as... just a second. You? Hold your breath, and one second stretches into a full year. A year to think. To remember. To plan. But there’s a rule. Your mind runs free. Your body doesn’t move. Not a finger. Not even your eyes. You can think anything... but you can’t do anything. Not until you breathe out. So what if one second felt like a year? The first time is a maths test. Question seven. You hold your breath... and you get a whole year with it. You solve it. Then you solve it three more ways. Then you get bored and invent a board game in your head. You breathe out. The clock hasn’t moved. Leo, you finished in four minutes? I had... plenty of time. Plus: chess club. Every move gets a year of thought. You beat the school champion in eleven moves. Your best friend Jake stares at you. How long were you thinking? About a second. Mate. That was a very scary second. Then football. A hard penalty reaches the goal in about half a second. You hold your breath as the ball leaves the boot. You see the spin. The angle. The exact corner. But the rule still holds. You can’t dive any faster than any other keeper. You just dive the right way... first time. Fingertips. Saved. Minus: your eyes can’t move. So for a whole year, you stare at one frozen picture. Sometimes it’s a sunset. Sometimes it’s Jake... mid-sneeze. For three hundred and sixty-five days. Your heart beats roughly once a second. So inside the year, you hear one heartbeat. Just one. Like a door closing very, very slowly. But where it gets really crazy... is a storm. A lightning flash is over in less than a second. You get to watch it crawl across the sky... for months. And there’s a cost. Every time you use it, you live another year. Alone. No voices. No touch. Just your thoughts and one frozen picture. You’re fifteen. But you’ve used it forty times. Which means... you’ve lived forty years nobody saw. The price? Scientists know long isolation is hard on the human brain. People kept alone for long stretches often report anxiety, confusion, even seeing things that aren’t there. You start to feel it. Jake notices. Dude. You sigh like my dad. And one question keeps growing. What if one day... you can’t make yourself breathe out? Only one person knows. Grandpa. He doesn’t laugh. He just nods. A whole year alone? Then don’t go in empty-handed. Take a song. His song. The same old disco record he dances to every Sunday, tapping the counter. He even taught you the steps. Boom. Boom. Boom. About a hundred beats a minute. You hum it inside every frozen year, so the silence isn’t silent. And whenever you worry, he says the same thing. A second is plenty, Leo... if you use it. Sunday. The kitchen. The song. Grandpa falls. And you hold your breath. Now you’re in the year. A body takes about half a second to reach the floor. You watch every centimeter. What can a frozen boy do? Nothing. Not yet. You remember a first-aid class. When a heart suddenly stops, the brain can start to be damaged after just a few minutes without oxygen. Every real second counts. And you have a whole year... to think about that. Can you even remember the steps? You go over that class again. And again. A thousand times. But in here, nothing gets worse. Grandpa’s still standing, sort of. Nothing ends. You could stay. A year. Ten years. Forever. In here, he’s still okay. Out there... the clock starts. Stay in the frozen second... or step out and fight for him. Then you hear it. One long note. His song. A second is plenty... if you use it. You’ve had your year. Now use the second. You breathe out. The spoon hits the floor. Mom! Call an ambulance! You kneel. Hands in the center of his chest. Push hard. About five centimeters deep. About a hundred to a hundred and twenty times a minute. How fast is that? You already know. Boom. Boom. Boom. His song is still playing. You push on every beat. Your arms burn. You don’t stop. Nine minutes later, sirens. The paramedics take over with a defibrillator. Then... a beep. A heartbeat. Who kept that rhythm going? Me. And his song. The next day, Grandpa smiles from a hospital bed. So. How long did that second feel? A year. And the nine minutes after? You squeeze his hand. Like nine minutes. Grandpa winks. Told you. A second is plenty. And for the first time in forty years... you’re not alone. That night, you hold your breath, just to rest. And in the frozen picture, something... moves. Someone else is in the year with you. Is any of this real? People often say time slowed down during a scare. In 2007, neuroscientist David Eagleman tested it. Volunteers fell about thirty meters into a net while watching a display flash numbers too fast to read. If time really slowed, they could read them. They couldn’t. Their brains just recorded more. Frightening moments get stored in richer detail, so they feel longer afterward. And the CPR part? That’s real.
