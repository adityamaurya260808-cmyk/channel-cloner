You can sit at

your desk for six hours

and get nothing done.

Or sit down for

ninety minutes and finish

a week of work...

...and not

even notice the

time passing.

Same brain.

Same desk.

Same person.

The difference is

not discipline,

talent, or motivation.

It is a set

of conditions psychologists have

measured for fifty years.

Your focus is not

broken. It is

being taxed every day.

Killingsworth and

Gilbert pinged

people's phones at

random moments.

Minds wandered

about 47% of

the time.

And the

wandering made them

less happy.

Today: five

laws of

deep focus.

I'm

PsychToons. Let's

begin.

An interruption

costs more than

the interruption.

Gloria Mark

tracked how

people work in

real offices.

It took over 23

minutes to fully

return to a task.

For a notification

that took four

seconds to read.

Sophie Leroy

called

it

attention residue.

You look at the new work,

but part of your mind

is still at the old one.

Checking 'just one

message' starts your work

with a handicap.

In 2004 people stayed

on a screen

~2.5 minutes before switching.

Today it's

about

47 seconds.

Your brain is not

weak. It's well trained

for the wrong thing.

Focus fails

at the first

small interruption you

let in.

Your phone drains

you even when

you ignore it.

'I'm fine, it's

just face down

on the desk.'

University of Texas, 2017:

an experiment on

attention and working memory.

One group's phone was in

another room, one on the

desk, one in a bag.

The group with

the phone in another

room scored best.

Even a silent phone

reduced cognitive capacity. Researchers

called it brain drain.

Part of your

brain spends energy

on not checking.

Resisting is work, paid

from the same

budget you think with.

The fix isn't

more willpower.

Remove the temptation.

Focus loves

the right level

of difficulty.

Csikszentmihalyi studied

surgeons,

chess

players, climbers.

He called

the absorbed

state: flow.

Flow happens when

challenge is slightly

above your skill.

Too easy:

boredom. Your

mind wanders.

Too hard:

anxiety. Your

mind escapes.

Between them, attention

locks in

on its own.

Ask: is this

too easy,

or too hard?

Too easy: add

a constraint.

Race a timer.

Too hard: shrink it

to a first step

you can already do.

Clarity

beats

intensity.

'Work on the

project.' Forty minutes

later: a reorganised folder

and two emails.

'Work on the project'

is not a

task. It's a fog.

Gollwitzer studied implementation intentions:

'When X happens,

I will do Y.'

People with a specific

plan were far more

likely to follow through.

Before each session write:

what will be finished

when the timer ends?

Not 'study chapter three.'

But: answer the

five end-questions without looking.

Vague tasks invite

distraction. Precise

tasks invite flow.

Focus is

a muscle that

needs recovery.

Anders Ericsson

studied

elite violinists.

The best practised intensely,

but only about

four hours a day.

In sessions of about

an hour, with

real rest in between.

Deep focus is

expensive. You can't

run it nonstop.

Work 50

to 90 minutes.

Then stop.

Walk. Stare out

of a window.

Not a feed.

A feed isn't

rest. It's different work

for your brain.

A tired brain

is a distractible

brain. Sleep matters.

The protocol:

phone in

another room.

One line:

what will

be finished?

A difficulty

that

stretches you.

Fifty to

ninety minutes.

No switching.

Rest

properly. Then

repeat.

After two weeks,

focus stops feeling

like a fight.

Csikszentmihalyi: the best moments

are when the mind is

stretched to its limit.

Deep focus isn't a trick.

It's the feeling of

a mind fully in use.

breaking most? Tell
