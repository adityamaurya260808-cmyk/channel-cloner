A wasp drifts toward your sandwich. You watch it come. Slowly. Like a feather sinking through honey. You have time to count its stripes... and step aside. Your mom drops a glass. You see it tip. You see it fall. You remember where the dustpan is... before it even reaches the floor. Your little brother blinks. To you, that blink takes ten seconds. Theo, why are you staring at me? Because, Sam... your eyelid is still closing. And your toast? It still hasn’t popped. You’ve been waiting for it for what feels like an hour. Your name is Theo. You’re fourteen. A normal person reacts to something they see in about a quarter of a second. You? Two and a half thousandths. A hundred times faster. Drop a ruler between most people’s fingers, and it falls about thirty centimeters before they grab it. Drop one for you... and your brain notices after three hundredths of a millimeter. The catch? Your brain got a hundred times faster. Your body did not. Your nerves still carry signals at about a hundred meters per second. Your legs still run like any fourteen-year-old’s. You see everything coming... you just can’t move any faster to meet it. So what if your reflexes were a hundred times faster? Plus: you never lose at catch. Your best friend Mia throws a ball at your face. You have time to yawn. Then you catch it. How... how did you even see that? I see everything, Mia. Okay. That’s actually creepy. Video games? You read every enemy before it moves. Forty wins in a row. Then the game bans you for cheating. Dude. Even the computer thinks you’re cheating. Movies? A film shows twenty-four pictures every second. Everyone else sees motion. You see... a slideshow. Click. Click. Click. Great movie, right? Very... clicky. Minus: some old lights flicker a hundred times a second. Nobody notices. You do. Your school hallway strobes like a nightclub. Then there’s track. Coach Lee fires the starting gun. You’re gone before the smoke clears. And disqualified. Leave the blocks less than a tenth of a second after the gun, and the rules decide you must have guessed. The best sprinters react in about fifteen hundredths. Nobody beats a tenth. Nobody... except you. So you learn the strangest skill in sport. Waiting. Coach Lee hands you a rule. Count, Theo. Count to what? Count until you’re on time. Fast doesn’t matter. On time does. But where it gets really crazy... is the road. A car at fifty kilometers per hour moves about fourteen meters every second. You see every one of those meters. You see drivers glance at phones. You see near-misses nobody else even notices. And there’s a cost. The price? Every second feels like a minute and forty seconds. A ten-minute lesson feels like more than sixteen hours. Mia says good morning... and you plan your answer, rewrite it twice, and get bored waiting for her to finish. Your mind lives a hundred days for every day you live. People start to feel like statues. Beautiful. And lonely. Usually both at once. Who talks to statues? And who... is going to wait for you? Sam does. Your seven-year-old brother. The only person who doesn’t think you’re weird. He waits for you at the school gate every afternoon. He listens to your endless stories. He never once says... hurry up. Years ago, you taught him a game. A whistle. Two notes. High, then low. It means: freeze. Whenever you whistle, he stops mid-step like a robot. Every single time. Even in the supermarket. Sam! Not in the cereal aisle! Robots don’t choose aisles, Theo. Sports day. The hundred-meter final. The track sits right beside the main road. Your whole family is watching. You crouch in the blocks. On your marks. Set. Your brain waits for the gun. And then... you see it. A red ball bouncing through the open gate. And a small boy chasing it. Sam. Down the road comes a delivery truck. Fifty kilometers per hour. Forty meters away. Under three seconds. You do the math... in a blink. You’re sixty meters from that gate. Even the fastest man in history needed over six seconds to cover sixty meters. You’d need eight. Your brain is fast. Your legs are not. You can’t reach him. And the gun hasn’t fired. Move now... and you’re out. Months of training. Gone. The race... or your brother. You stand up. Before the gun. The crowd groans. False start. But you don’t run. Running won’t work. Instead, you whistle. Two notes. High, then low. Sound travels at about three hundred and forty meters per second. It reaches Sam in less than a fifth of a second. You watch the sound arrive. You watch him hear it. And Sam... freezes. One foot in the air. Right at the curb. The truck rushes past. The ball rolls under its wheels. Pop. Silence. Then Sam turns around... and waves. The whole stadium saw it. Coach Lee walks over. You know you’re disqualified, right? I know. Fast doesn’t matter. On time does. Mia is crying. Your mom is running. And Sam crashes into your arms. For once, you don’t mind that every second lasts so long. You want this one... to last forever. That night, a letter slides under your door. No name. Just one line. ‘We saw what you did on that road. We would like to test you.’ Is any of this real? Partly. Human visual reaction really sits around a quarter of a second. Sprinting really does treat anything under a tenth of a second as a false start. And some animals really do see faster than us. Houseflies notice flicker that looks like smooth light to you, which helps explain why they’re so hard to swat. But a human brain a hundred times faster? Your nerves are simply too slow to keep up.
