TOPIC: What If You Had 1,000 IQ?
SCRIPT: You look at a Rubik’s cube. Once. You hand it back... solved. You read a physics textbook over breakfast. Then you find a mistake on page two hundred. Your dad’s car makes a strange noise. You tell him which part is broken... from the kitchen. How... how did you know that? It told me. And your little sister still beats you... at hide and seek. Every single time. Your name is Kai. You’re thirteen. Most people score around a hundred on an IQ test. Fewer than one in seven hundred score above one hundred and forty-five. You? One thousand. No test can even measure that. Most stop working well before one hundred and sixty. But there’s a rule. Your brain got smarter. Your body didn’t. Your brain is only about two percent of your weight... yet it burns about twenty percent of your energy. Running at full power, yours burns far more. Push it too hard... and it shuts down. So what if you had a thousand IQ? Plus: school becomes a joke. You finish the year’s maths in a weekend. Then the next five years. Your teacher stares at your homework. Kai... I don’t understand your answer. That’s okay. I wrote you a simpler one. Languages? You learn Spanish on Saturday. Japanese on Sunday. Your best friend Ben is still learning his times tables. Show-off. Chess? You beat the town champion blindfolded. While eating cereal. The world record for solving a Rubik’s cube is around three seconds. You don’t time yourself. It feels rude. Then everyone wants something. Your uncle wants lottery numbers. Kai, just one lucky guess? Lotteries are random, Uncle. That’s the whole point. Then you fix something real. The town’s traffic lights are badly timed. You rewrite the pattern in one afternoon. Rush hour gets twelve minutes shorter. Nobody knows it was you. Which... is kind of nice. Minus: the headaches. Think too fast for too long, and your skull starts to pound. Your brain has about eighty-six billion neurons. Yours fire like they’re all late for something. You sleep twelve hours a night. And you’re always hungry. Is that your third sandwich? Fourth... Brains are expensive. But where it gets really crazy... is the weather. You start predicting storms better than the forecast. A scientist named Dr. Lee notices. She invites you to the city’s flood center. How did you know it would rain on Tuesday? The clouds told me. You just weren’t listening. She doesn’t laugh. She gives you a desk. And there’s a cost. The price? People stop seeing a kid. They see a calculator. Teachers ask you for answers. Adults ask you for money. Even Ben stops joking around. Mate... are you even still you? You don’t know. Everything feels slow. Conversations feel like waiting for a bus. Mom finds you awake at three in the morning, staring at the ceiling. What are you thinking about, sweetheart? Everything. All at once. It’s loud in here. And the more you use your brain, the more it hurts. Dr. Lee warns you. Full power for too long... and you might not come back the same. Being smart is easy, you tell her. Being useful is the hard part. Only one person treats you like nothing changed. Your little sister, Mia. She’s eight. She thinks your brain is boring. She wins at hide and seek because you let her. And she draws you every week with a tiny little head. I like you better dumb. Every Friday, you two lie on the grass and guess shapes in the clouds. You could name every cloud type in Latin. You don’t. That one’s a dragon, Mia says. Definitely a dragon. When storms come, you taught her one rhyme. Turn around... don’t drown. If water’s on the road, you don’t walk through it. You go up. Moving water just fifteen centimeters deep can knock an adult off their feet. Thirty centimeters can float a car. Mia rolls her eyes. I know, Kai. Up. Not through. Then comes the storm. Three days of rain. At the flood center, every screen is blue. The river is rising. The water is climbing about ten centimeters every hour. The official forecast says the old levee will hold. You look at the numbers... and your stomach drops. It won’t. Somewhere along twelve kilometers of wall, it’s going to break. But where? And when? Can you even be sure? To find out, you’d have to run every piece of data at once. Full power. The one thing Dr. Lee told you never to do. Then your phone buzzes. Mia’s school is on a field trip today. To the riverside museum. Right beside the levee. You call Mia’s teacher. No answer. The storm has knocked out half the phone towers. Buses would need twenty minutes to reach the museum. If the wall breaks first... there’s no time at all. You could stay safe. Keep your thousand IQ. Hope the forecast is right. Or you could push your brain past its limit... and maybe lose it forever. Dr. Lee grabs your arm. Kai. If you do this, I can’t promise what comes back. What’s the point of a brain... if it just watches? Keep the gift... or keep your sister. Being smart is easy. Being useful is the hard part. You close your eyes. And you open the floodgates in your head. Every rain gauge. Every crack report since 2007. Every bend in the river. Your head burns. The room tilts. But the answer arrives. Section nine. Thirty-eight minutes. Right behind the museum. Nobody listens to a thirteen-year-old with a hunch. So you don’t give them a hunch. You give them proof. The man in charge folds his arms. The forecast says it holds. The forecast is using last year’s data, you say. The river moved. You show him the pressure numbers. The old crack nobody fixed. Dr. Lee checks it... and goes pale. Evacuate. Now. Then the floor rushes up to meet you. Everything goes dark. You wake up two days later. Mom is crying. Ben is holding a sandwich, just in case. You did it, mate. You’re still you. The levee broke at section nine... thirty-eight minutes later. Nobody was hurt. And Mia? When the alarm went off, she grabbed her teacher’s hand and shouted one thing. Not through! Up! Her whole class climbed to the museum’s top floor. Then Dr. Lee hands you a test. Your new IQ? One hundred and thirty-two. Still clever. Just... human. Mia hands you a new drawing. Your head is normal-sized now. Much better. And for the first time in months, Ben’s jokes feel fast enough to be funny. That Friday, you lie on the grass with Mia. Look, she says. A dragon. You squint. You can’t remember a single cloud name in Latin. Definitely a dragon. Being smart is easy. Being useful is the hard part. Is any of this real? IQ tests compare you with other people your age, so a score of a thousand doesn’t really mean anything. Your brain really does use about a fifth of your body’s energy. Though, honestly, hard thinking only burns a little extra. And the flood advice is completely real. Never walk or drive through moving water. Go up, not through.
